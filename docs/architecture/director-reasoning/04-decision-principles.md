# 04 — Decision Principles

How Director Reasoning weighs and arrives at judgment. These describe the **modes of decision** reasoning must be capable of — what it means to prioritize, trade off, evaluate, and communicate confidence well. They are principles of good judgment, not algorithms; no method, formula, or mechanism is specified.

Every mode below serves the [first principles](02-first-principles.md) and produces advisory output only ([03 — Reasoning Boundaries](03-reasoning-boundaries.md)).

## Prioritization

Reasoning must rank. Faced with many possible focuses, it judges which matter most to the organization *now* — by importance, urgency, and fit to direction. Good prioritization surfaces the few things worth the Director's attention and sets aside the rest, without discarding them. It answers *"of everything we could attend to, what should we?"* — and shows why.

## Trade-off analysis

Real decisions have costs on every side. Reasoning must make trade-offs **explicit** — what is gained, what is given up, for each option — rather than presenting a favored choice as cost-free. Honest trade-off analysis lets the Director weigh the same tensions reasoning weighed. Hiding a trade-off to make a recommendation look cleaner is a failure of both truth and explainability.

## Opportunity evaluation

Reasoning judges opportunities on their merits and their fit: is this worth pursuing, for *this* organization, given its context, capabilities, and history? Evaluation grounds each opportunity in evidence ([memory](../memory/README.md), the graph) and states the case honestly — including when a tempting opportunity is, on examination, not worth it.

## Risk evaluation

Every recommendation carries risk, and reasoning names it. Risk evaluation asks *what could go wrong, how likely, how bad, and how recoverable* — and weighs it against the potential gain. Reasoning neither ignores risk to make an option attractive nor inflates it to avoid a decision; it reports risk as plainly as opportunity.

## Organizational impact

Reasoning judges every option by its effect on the **whole organization**, not just its immediate target — using the relationship graph to trace how a change ripples ([impact analysis](../relationship-graph/05-impact-analysis.md)). A recommendation that looks good locally but harms the organization fails on impact. Reasoning is responsible for seeing the second- and third-order effects the Director might not.

## Confidence communication

Reasoning tells the Director *how sure it is*. Every judgment carries an honest signal of confidence — firm where the evidence is strong, tentative where it is thin, with the gaps named ([explicit uncertainty](02-first-principles.md)). Confidence communication is what lets the Director calibrate how much weight to put on a recommendation. A recommendation without honest confidence is a recommendation the Director cannot properly weigh.

---

## The shape of a well-reasoned judgment

A judgment that honors these principles arrives at the Director as:

- **A ranked focus** — this matters, here's why, relative to the rest.
- **An honest set of options** — each with its trade-offs, opportunity, and risk laid out.
- **An organizational read** — how each option affects the whole, not just the part.
- **A calibrated recommendation** — a clear suggestion, with its confidence stated and its reasoning explainable.

That package is reasoning's product. It is complete, honest, and advisory — everything the Director needs to decide well, and nothing that decides for them.
